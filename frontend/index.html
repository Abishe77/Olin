GRAVITATIONAL LENSING · INVERSE INFERENCE

Understanding the mass behind distorted light.

Gravitational lensing allows the matter between an astronomical observer and a distant source to be investigated through its effect on light. Olin explores this relationship as an inverse problem, using deep learning to estimate selected physical parameters from lensed astronomical images and examining those estimates through a physical forward model.

THE SCIENCE

When gravity changes the path of light.

Light does not always travel along the straight path that an observer might expect. According to general relativity, the gravitational field of a massive object curves the surrounding spacetime, changing the paths followed by light passing through it. When a distant astronomical source lies behind such an object, the foreground mass can act as a gravitational lens.

Depending on the geometry of the source, lens, and observer, the background object may appear magnified, distorted into arcs, or divided into multiple images. In favourable configurations, these images can form nearly complete rings around the foreground lens. These structures are not simply visual distortions. Their shapes and positions contain information about the gravitational potential responsible for bending the light.

This makes strong gravitational lensing an important way to investigate matter that cannot be understood from the light it emits alone. The observed image, however, does not directly reveal the physical configuration of the lens. Several different combinations of physical parameters can produce similar image structures. The task of inferring the underlying parameters from an observed lensed image is therefore an inverse problem.

THE INVERSE PROBLEM

From an observed image to a physical model.

The forward problem begins with a description of the lens and the background source. Given their physical parameters, a gravitational-lensing model calculates how light is deflected and produces a corresponding image. In a simulated experiment, these parameters are known before the observation is generated.

The inverse problem works in the opposite direction. An astronomical image is available, but the parameters that produced it are unknown. A model must use the observable structure of the image to estimate those parameters. This mapping is difficult because information about the physical system is compressed into a two-dimensional observation, and different configurations may produce similar appearances.

Olin investigates this inverse mapping using a supervised deep-learning model. Its training observations are generated from a parameterised gravitational-lensing forward model. Because the physical parameters are known during generation, the resulting images can be paired with their corresponding parameter values. This provides controlled examples from which the inverse model can learn.

For an uploaded observation, the image is first prepared using the preprocessing conventions established during model development. A trained ResNet-18 model then estimates five selected parameters: the Einstein radius (θE), two components of lens ellipticity (e1 and e2), and two components of external shear (γ1 and γ2). The model also provides uncertainty estimates associated with its predictions.

These estimates describe a particular parameterisation of the lens model. They do not represent an unrestricted recovery of the complete mass distribution or the entire three-dimensional physical state of the astronomical system. The inference is meaningful within the assumptions and parameter range of the model used by Olin.

PHYSICAL RECONSTRUCTION

Examining an estimate through the forward model.

A numerical prediction alone does not show how well an inferred physical configuration explains an observation. To examine the result from a second perspective, Olin passes the estimated parameters back into its gravitational-lensing forward model. The model uses these values to generate a corresponding lensed image.

This generated image is the physics-based reconstruction. It can be compared directly with the uploaded observation to examine similarities and differences in their image structures. The residual represents the difference between the observation and the reconstruction, making discrepancies visible rather than reducing the analysis to a single prediction value.

The reconstruction is conditional on the physical model and its assumptions. A close visual match does not independently establish that every inferred parameter is uniquely correct, just as a discrepancy does not identify its cause by itself. Parameter degeneracies, observational effects, and limitations in the forward model can all influence the comparison.

ANALYSIS AND VALIDATION

Inspecting the inference, its uncertainty, and its limits.

An Olin analysis brings together the estimated physical parameters, their calibrated uncertainty, the reconstructed observation, and the residual between the reconstruction and the input image. Each output provides a different perspective on the inferred configuration. Together, they allow a user to examine what the model estimates and how that estimate behaves when evaluated through the physical forward model.

The results are assembled into a scientific PDF report containing the uploaded observation and the relevant analysis outputs. Completed analyses and their reports can be revisited through the authenticated application.

An important distinction remains between controlled synthetic evaluation and performance on real astronomical observations. Synthetic images provide known parameter values against which predictions can be measured. Real observations introduce additional complexities, and their complete underlying physical configurations are generally not known. Performance on synthetic data therefore does not, by itself, establish accuracy on every real astronomical dataset.

Olin is developed within a defined modelling regime. Its purpose is to investigate and deliver a constrained physics-grounded inverse-inference workflow, with the inferred parameters examined through the same physical modelling framework that gives rise to the simulated observations.
